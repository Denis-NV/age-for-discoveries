POST CAPTION (English):
Most people come to Tomar for the Manueline window or the round Templar church. Fewer make it through the door at the far end — into the part where the knights actually lived.

A long dormitory corridor with a vaulted wooden ceiling and rows of cell doors. Each room barely larger than a prison cell: plank floor, bare walls, a stone window seat that was probably also a desk. The view from it: a formal cloister garden below. In the grander rooms — the chapter house, the refectory with its stone tables worn smooth over centuries — you get a different sense of scale. This was a working institution that housed men who were simultaneously monks, soldiers, and bankers, and eventually the engine behind the biggest maritime expansion in history.

I keep thinking about those doors. The rooms behind them would have been dark in winter. And somewhere in those walls, the men who slept here decided to build ships and sail south to see what was at the end of the world.
